4--Weighting and Estimation 

Explain story point estimation techniques 

Story point estimation is a technique used in Agile and Scrum to estimate the relative effort, complexity, and risk of implementing a user story or feature. Unlike time-based estimation, story points are abstract units that allow teams to compare tasks without committing to exact hours. 

1. Planning Poker (Scrum Poker) 

2. T-Shirt Sizing 

3. Bucket System 

4. Affinity Estimation 

5. Dot Voting 

6. Three-Point Estimation (PERT-inspired) 

#Describe methods like Planning Poker, T-shirt sizing, etc. 

1. Planning Poker 

Method: 

Each team member is given a deck of cards with values (e.g., Fibonacci sequence: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13…). 

The Product Owner describes a user story. 

Each team member privately selects a card representing their estimate of effort. 

Everyone reveals their card simultaneously. 

Differences are discussed, especially if someone chose an extreme value. 

Repeat until consensus or near-consensus is reached. 

Pros: 

Encourages discussion and knowledge sharing. 

Reduces anchoring and bias because estimates are revealed simultaneously. 

Cons: 

Can take time for large backlogs. 

 

2. T-shirt Sizing 

Method: 

Instead of numerical values, stories are sized like T-shirts: XS, S, M, L, XL. 

The team discusses each story and assigns a size relative to other stories. 

Later, these sizes can be mapped to story points if needed. 

Pros: 

Simple and intuitive for beginners. 

Works well for high-level estimations. 

Cons: 

Less precise than numerical methods. 

 

3. Dot Voting / Affinity Estimation 

Method: 

Stories are placed on a wall or board. 

Team members place dots or stickers to indicate the size or priority of each story. 

Affinity estimation involves grouping stories into similar sizes (small, medium, large) quickly, then refining relative sizes. 

Pros: 

Fast and visual. 

Good for large sets of stories. 

Cons: 

Can be less rigorous; depends on team’s experience. 

 

4. Bucket System 

Method: 

Stories are placed into “buckets” representing story points (e.g., 1, 2, 3, 5, 8…). 

The team quickly decides which bucket each story belongs to, sometimes using rounds for adjustments. 

Pros: 

Efficient for estimating many stories. 

Maintains relative sizing. 

Cons: 

Needs team familiarity with story points. 

 

5. Three-Point Estimation (PERT) 

Method: 

Each story is given three estimates: 

Optimistic (O) – best-case scenario 

Most Likely (M) – most realistic scenario 

Pessimistic (P) – worst-case scenario 

Formula for expected estimate: 

Estimate=O+4M+P6\text{Estimate} = \frac{O + 4M + P}{6}Estimate=6O+4M+P   

Pros: 

Incorporates uncertainty. 

Provides more accurate, weighted estimates. 

Cons: 

Slightly more complex; needs understanding of probability. 

 

#Discuss how to weight user stories by complexity, priority, and business value 

1. Weighting by Complexity (Effort or Difficulty) 

Complexity estimates how much work a story will require. It helps the team understand effort, risk, and resource allocation. 

Common methods: 

Story Points: A relative measure of effort, usually using a Fibonacci sequence (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13…). 

Small tasks = low points. 

Large, complex tasks = high points. 

T-shirt sizing: Small, Medium, Large, XL. 

Planning Poker: Team members independently estimate effort, then discuss discrepancies. 

Why it matters: 
Stories with high complexity might take longer, need specialized skills, or introduce more risk. Weighting by complexity ensures realistic sprint planning. 

 

2. Weighting by Priority 

Priority reflects urgency or importance to the customer or business. 

How to determine priority: 

MoSCoW method: Must have, Should have, Could have, Won’t have. 

Stakeholder input: Product Owner consults clients, business analysts, and key users. 

Dependency mapping: Some features may need to be implemented before others. 

Why it matters: 
High-priority stories should generally be scheduled before lower-priority ones, even if they are less complex, to maximize immediate value or meet deadlines. 

 

3. Weighting by Business Value 

Business value measures the impact of a story on the product or organization. 

Techniques: 

Value points: Assign numeric values (e.g., 1–10) based on potential revenue, customer satisfaction, or market impact. 

Kano model: Classifies features as Must-have, Performance, or Delighters. 

Cost of Delay (CoD): Quantifies potential loss if a story is delayed. 

Why it matters: 
High-value stories give the greatest return on investment. A story with moderate complexity but high business value may be prioritized over a low-value, low-complexity story. 

 

4. Combining the Three Factors 

Many teams use a weighted scoring model to balance complexity, priority, and value: 

Example formula: 

Weighted Score=Business Value×PriorityComplexity\text{Weighted Score} = \frac{\text{Business Value} \times \text{Priority}}{\text{Complexity}}Weighted Score=ComplexityBusiness Value×Priority   

High business value + high priority → higher score. 

High complexity → reduces the score slightly (if desired to avoid bottlenecks). 

Allows objective comparison across stories. 

Steps: 

Assign each story a value for complexity, priority, and business value. 

Normalize scales (e.g., 1–5 for each factor). 

Calculate the weighted score. 

Rank stories by score for backlog refinement and sprint planning. 

 

5. Practical Tips 

Always involve the team in estimates for better accuracy. 

Reassess weights regularly; priorities and business value can change. 

Consider risk and dependencies along with weighted scores. 

Use visual tools like weighted scoring matrices to simplify decisions.
